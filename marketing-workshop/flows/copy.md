# Copy Flow

Eight steps, roughly 35 minutes, plus a ninth that only runs if the session
can actually publish. Output is a rewritten hero section, one more asset of
their choosing, a page that can actually be found — by search and by models
answering buying questions — and, where possible, that page live.

Run one step per turn. See the pacing section in `SKILL.md`.

**Requires a positioning section in the brief.** If it's missing, run the express
pass at the end of the positioning flow first — three questions, ten minutes. Copy written without a segment and a value proposition comes out fluent
and says nothing, which is the condition most homepages are already in.

Open by telling them the shape: audit what they have, rewrite the top of the
page, pick one more asset, make it findable, then — if there's a way to
actually publish it — ship it. Warn them that step 1 stings.

**Where the copy goes.** Each asset gets its own draft — the homepage draft, the
ad set, the email sequence — kept separate from the brief. The brief gets the
hero section and the decisions, because that's the part later flows and other
people need to read; long copy in the brief buries the header.

---

## Step 1 — Audit against the position

**Ask:** Paste your current homepage — headline, subhead, and the first section
below it. A URL works too.

Then run it against the brief, in this order:

- Does the headline name the segment, or could any company in the category run
  it unchanged?
- Is the differentiator from the value proposition on the page at all? Usually
  it isn't — it was decided forty minutes ago and the page predates it.
- Count claims with evidence attached against claims without.
- Read the first sentence aloud. Does it match the voice rules, or the category?

Give the verdict in about five lines. This is a diagnosis, not a rewrite — the
rewrite is the next three steps, and doing it here skips the part where they see
the gap themselves.

**Push back on:** the urge to be kind about it. The gap between the position they
just built and the page they already have is the most useful thing in the flow.
Name it plainly, then move.

If someone has no site yet, ask for their current one-line description instead —
the email signature, the deck slide, whatever they say at events. Audit that.

**Write to brief:** three bullets on what the current page gets wrong.

---

## Step 2 — Hero section

The hero is the headline, the subhead, and the button. It carries most of the
page's work, so it gets its own step.

**Ask them to draft the headline first**, then tighten together. Same rule as the
value proposition: a headline you wrote is one you can defend in a meeting on
Thursday.

Give them three shapes to choose between, and write one of each:

1. **Plain** — says what it is, for whom. Low ceiling, no downside.
   *Invoicing for agencies that bill hourly.*
2. **Outcome** — the state the customer ends up in. Needs to be specific enough
   to be falsifiable.
3. **Against the alternative** — pointed at what's currently winning. Highest
   risk, highest recall, only works if the hole taken in positioning step 4 is
   real.

Have them pick one and say why. The subhead carries what the headline dropped —
usually the segment or the mechanism, never both.

The CTA names the next action, not the emotion. "Start free trial" beats "Get
started". If the action is a demo call, say so; people bounce on ambiguity more
than on friction.

**Push back on:** headlines that could belong to a competitor, abstract nouns
doing the work ("clarity", "simplicity"), and any sentence that needs the subhead
to make sense.

**Write to brief:** the chosen hero — headline, subhead, CTA — plus the two
rejected headlines. Start the homepage draft with it.

---

## Step 3 — The body

**Ask:** nothing yet. The three ranked points from positioning step 6 are the
body. Read them back and confirm the order still holds now that the hero exists.

Then, one section per point, in rank order:

- A section heading that makes a claim, not a label. "Set up in an afternoon"
  beats "Onboarding".
- Two or three sentences.
- The evidence attached to that point — the number, the customer outcome, the
  screenshot. Evidence goes next to the claim, not on a separate page.

One idea per section. The most common homepage failure is a page that says nine
things at equal volume, which reads as saying nothing. If they want a fourth
point, ask which of the three it replaces.

**Push back on:** sections that describe features the reader hasn't been given a
reason to care about yet. Order is claim, then evidence, then mechanism — never
mechanism first.

**Write to:** the homepage draft.

---

## Step 4 — Objections on the page

**Ask:** Of the objections in your brief, which ones stop people before they ever
talk to you?

Those are the ones the page has to handle. The rest belong in a sales
conversation, and putting them on the page raises doubts most readers didn't
have.

Handle them in the format that fits:

- **Price** — a pricing section, or at minimum a range. Hiding price reads as
  expensive, and for a self-serve product it's the top reason people leave.
- **Trust and size** — customer names, numbers, a founder's name and face. "Too
  small to bet on" is answered by specificity, not by sounding corporate.
- **Switching cost** — say what migration actually involves. An honest "about two
  hours, we do the import" outperforms silence.
- **The rest** — an FAQ, phrased as the question a real person asks, answered in
  the voice rules and without deflection.

**Push back on:** answers that dodge. If the objection is "you're too new", the
page can't claim to be established; it has to make new acceptable — a guarantee,
a short contract, a named reference.

**Write to:** the homepage draft. Note in the brief which objections went on the
page and which were held back for sales.

---

## Step 5 — One more asset

**Ask:** Which of these is the thing you'd actually ship this week?

Offer four. Do one properly rather than four badly — this step is five minutes,
and a half-written email sequence gets deleted the moment the workshop ends.

**A. Landing page** — for one campaign or one segment, not the whole company.
Differences from the homepage: one offer, one CTA repeated, no navigation, and
the headline echoes the ad or email that sent them there. Message match is the
whole game; a landing page that doesn't repeat the ad's promise loses most of the
traffic it paid for. Ask what sends traffic to it before writing a word.

**B. Ads** — write five headlines across three angles: the pain, the outcome, and
the alternative they're currently using. Same offer, different door. Keep each
under the platform's limit and check the landing page repeats the winning line.
Tell them what they're testing — angle first, wording second. Testing five
wordings of one angle teaches nothing.

**C. Email sequence** — four emails: the one that delivers what they signed up
for, the one that names the problem, the one that shows the mechanism or a
customer, and the one that asks. One idea and one link per email. Subject lines
last, written after the body, from the sentence in the email that's actually
interesting. Say the send spacing out loud — the sequence is a schedule, not a
folder of drafts.

**D. One-pager, bio, or listing** — the compressed forms: a leave-behind PDF, a
founder bio, a directory or marketplace listing, a cold page for a specific
account. All of them are the value proposition plus three points plus proof,
fitted to a box. Listings especially: most directories rank on the first sixty
characters, so the segment goes in early and the cleverness goes nowhere.

**Write to:** the asset's own draft, and one line in the brief naming which asset
was built.

---

## Step 6 — Keywords and topics

**Ask:** What would someone type before they knew you existed?

That framing matters. Founders search for their own category name; buyers search
for their problem, or for a competitor, or for "how do I X". Get ten phrases and
sort them into three intents:

- **Problem** — describing the symptom, no solution in mind. Highest volume,
  slowest to convert, best for content.
- **Category** — shopping for a class of tool. This is where the money is and
  where competition is hardest.
- **Comparison and alternative** — "X vs Y", "alternatives to X", "X pricing".
  Small volume, high intent, and consistently the most under-served.

Search to check the phrases are real and see who currently ranks. Don't invent
volume numbers — a made-up figure in front of a room is worse than no figure, and
the shape of the results page tells you more anyway. If the first page is all
listicles, that's a different opportunity than if it's all vendors.

**Push back on:** vanity terms. Ranking for the category head term is a
multi-year project; ranking for "alternatives to [the incumbent]" is a page they
can write this afternoon.

**Write to brief:** the ten phrases grouped by intent, and the three they're
going after first.

---

## Step 7 — On-page pass

Apply this to the page written in steps 2–4, not as general advice.

- **Title tag** — the primary phrase and the brand, under about sixty
  characters. This is what shows in results and it's not the H1.
- **Meta description** — a reason to click, not a summary. It doesn't affect
  ranking; it affects whether the ranking is worth anything.
- **H1** — one per page, and it should be the headline they chose, not a
  keyword-stuffed variant. If the keyword won't fit the headline naturally, it
  goes in an H2.
- **One topic per page.** The most common fix is splitting a page that's trying
  to rank for four unrelated things into four pages.
- **Internal links** — the new page should be linked from somewhere, with link
  text that says what's on the other side.
- **Alt text** on the images that carry meaning, skipped on the decorative ones.
- **The URL** — short, readable, and never changed later without a redirect.

**Push back on:** anything that fights the voice rules. A sentence bent around a
keyword reads bent, and the ranking isn't worth the sentence.

**Write to:** the top of the homepage draft, as a metadata block.

---

## Step 8 — Visibility in AI answers

A growing share of buying research now happens inside a model's answer rather
than on a results page, and the reader never sees a list of links. Two things
decide whether the product shows up there.

**Be extractable.** Models quote pages that state things plainly:

- Answer the question in the first sentence of the section, then explain.
- Say what the product is and who it's for in one unambiguous sentence — a model
  can't infer a segment from a mood board.
- Publish the pages nobody wants to write: pricing, comparisons, limitations, a
  page that says who it's *not* for. These are disproportionately what gets
  quoted, because they're the questions being asked.
- Keep facts consistent everywhere they appear. A model reconciling three
  different pricing claims will hedge or skip.
- Structured data (Organization, Product, FAQPage) where the site supports it.
  Worth doing, not worth an hour of the workshop.

**Be cited elsewhere.** What third parties say weighs more than what the site
says — directories, comparison roundups, forum threads, the listings from step 5.
This is the honest link between this step and the distribution flow, and it's why
distribution comes next rather than being optional.

**The test:** ask a model the question a buyer would ask — "best X for Y" — and
read the answer together. Whatever it names instead of them is the real
competitive set, and it's often not the ladder drawn in positioning step 2.

**Push back on:** anyone wanting tricks. Prompt-injection text hidden on a page
is worthless and gets the domain distrusted. There is no shortcut here; the work
is clear pages and third-party mentions.

**Write to brief:** what the model said, and the two pages they need to publish.

---

## Step 9 — Ship it, if the session can

Everything above produces copy a person can publish by hand. If this
session also has real write access to wherever the page lives — a CMS, a
site repo, a page builder — and the person wants it live now, this step
does it, with the same care any publish deserves:

Show the exact destination and the exact final content, once, in full.
Get an explicit yes to that specific preview — not a general "sounds good"
given earlier in the conversation. Then publish it, then **read the live
page back** and confirm it actually matches what was approved before
saying it's done. If the read-back doesn't match, or the publish fails,
say so plainly and don't retry more than once.

**If there's no write access to publish it:** the copy above plus the
implementation checklist from step 5 is the complete deliverable — say so
plainly, and never describe it as live. A page that's written and ready is
a real, useful result; it just isn't the same claim as a page that's
actually up.

**Write to brief:** whether the asset is live (with what was verified) or
ready to hand off, and to whom.

---

## Closing the flow

Read back the hero section only, and whether it's live or ready to publish.
The files hold the rest.

Then the checkpoint: tell them to post their new headline where the room can see
it — and next to it, the one it replaced. The pair is more interesting than the
headline alone, and it's the best material for show and tell.

Then what's next: the distribution flow takes this copy and works out where it
goes. If they built ads or a one-pager in step 5, note that distribution will
tell them which channels those actually belong in — some of the best ones don't
accept either.
