# Positioning Flow

Eight steps, roughly 40 minutes. The method is Ries and Trout's, from
*Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* — positioning as something you do to
the mind of the prospect, not to the product.

> "Positioning is not what you do to a product. Positioning is what you do to
> the mind of the prospect."

That sentence decides the shape of every step below. The questions are not
about what the product is or what the founder wants it to be. They're about
what is already in the customer's head, what's already occupying the space,
and what can realistically be moved. A founder answering from inside their own
product will produce a position nobody outside the company can hold in mind.

Run one step per turn. See the pacing section in `SKILL.md` — it matters more
here than anywhere else, because these steps only work if the person is doing
the thinking.

Open by telling them the shape: eight questions, the first three are about the
prospect's mind rather than their product, and what they're building towards is
a position short enough to survive in a crowded head.

---

## Step 1 — The position you own now

**Ask:** If I stopped one of your customers in the street and asked what you
are, what would they say? Not your pitch — their words.

Ries and Trout's first question in Chapter 21 is "What position do you own?",
and the whole trick is that it's answered by looking into the prospect's mind,
not out from the company's. Most founders answer with what they wish were true.

Get at it sideways if the direct question produces marketing language:
- What did the last three customers say when they explained you to someone else?
- What do people consistently get wrong about you?
- What's the one line in support tickets or sales calls that keeps recurring?

**Push back on:** the rehearsed pitch, and any answer with more than one idea
in it. A position that takes two sentences to state isn't held in anyone's mind
in two sentences.

*They say:* "We're an AI-powered workflow platform for modern teams."
*Weak:* "What does the platform do?" — a reasonable question that accepts the
frame and buys you two more paragraphs of the same pitch.
*Better:* "That's what's on your site. What did your last customer say when
their boss asked why they'd bought it?" — same topic, different head.

**Write to brief:** Product (header), the offer in plain words, and the
position they currently own — including "none" if that's the honest answer,
which for a young company it usually is.

---

## Step 2 — The ladder

**Ask:** What category does your customer file you under, and who else is on
that list?

The book's central image: the mind holds a ladder per category, and each brand
occupies a rung. You don't get to pick your ladder — the prospect assigns it.
Ask which one they're on, then get the rungs in order, top first.

Miller's rule of seven applies: a person holds around seven brands in a
category, fewer in one they don't care about. In most categories a customer
can name two or three. If the person lists twelve competitors, ask which three
their buyer could name unprompted — that's the real ladder.

**Then ask the alternative question:** when someone decides not to buy from
you, what do they do instead? This surfaces the rungs that aren't companies —
a spreadsheet, an intern, doing nothing. For a young product the top rung is
very often not a competitor at all, and a position built against a rival
nobody was considering is aimed at empty air.

If they don't know the landscape, search rather than working from memory —
naming a company that pivoted or shut down in front of a room costs
credibility.

**Write to brief:** Category (header), the ladder in rank order with the
non-product alternatives on it, and which rung they occupy today.

---

## Step 3 — The leader's word

**Ask:** What one word or idea does the top of that ladder own?

Ries and Trout are blunt about what follows: the easiest way into a mind is to
be first, and if someone else got there first, that word is gone. You cannot
take it by being better at it. Volvo owns safety; a second company arguing it
is safer is buying advertising for Volvo.

Two rules from the book to state plainly here:
- **You can't own the word the leader owns.** Not with a better product, not
  with a bigger budget.
- **The leader's strength is also the constraint.** What the leader is
  famous for is what they can't easily abandon, and that fixity is what makes
  a hole findable in step 4.

If they are the leader — genuinely first in the mind, not just first to
launch — the strategy inverts: hold the word, cover competitive moves quickly,
and advertise the category rather than the brand. Note it and move on; most
rooms have one such business at most.

**Push back on:** "nobody really owns it." Sometimes true, usually a sign the
ladder in step 2 was drawn from the founder's knowledge rather than the
customer's.

*They say:* "Honestly nobody owns anything here, the whole category is
undifferentiated."
*Weak:* "Then there's a real opportunity for you." — flattering, and it hands
them a conclusion the evidence doesn't support yet.
*Better:* "Say your category out loud to someone outside your company and ask
which name comes to mind first. If one comes back, they own something — being
the default is a word, and it's a hard one to take."

**Write to brief:** the leader, the word they own, and whether it's takeable
(it usually isn't).

---

## Step 4 — Cherchez le creneau

*Look for the hole.* This is the step the previous three exist to make
possible, and it's where most of the 40 minutes should go if time gets tight.

**Ask:** What's the gap on that ladder that nobody is standing in?

Work the holes the book names, out loud, one at a time — they're more useful
as a checklist than as a concept:

- **Size.** Volkswagen took "small" while Detroit sold long and low. Think
  small; think enormous.
- **High price.** A legitimate position where the product genuinely supports
  it and the category has no premium rung.
- **Low price.** Riskier — the leader can usually follow you down.
- **Who it's for.** Gender, age, profession, situation. Marlboro became the
  cigarette for men in a category that had been selling to everyone.
- **Time.** The one used at night, at the start of the quarter, on the first
  day of a project.
- **Distribution.** Where it's bought, not what it is.
- **Heavy user.** The 20% who account for most of the volume.

Then the two traps, both of which will show up in a room of founders:

- **The technology trap.** A real technical achievement with no corresponding
  hole in the mind. The engineering is genuine and nobody was waiting for it.
  Ask: is this a hole in the market, or a hole in your product roadmap?
- **The everybody trap.** Trying to be for everyone. "Small businesses" and
  "marketing teams" are the everybody trap wearing a segment's clothes. A
  position that excludes nobody occupies nothing.

**Then ask the cut:** who are you explicitly *not* for? Get one named group
they'll turn away. If they can't name one, they haven't found a hole yet.

**Push back on:** breadth, always. It's the most common failure in the flow and
the book's most repeated warning.

*They say:* "Our segment is small businesses."
*Weak:* "What kind of small businesses?" — sounds like a probe, but it accepts
the frame and gets you "small businesses in retail", which is the everybody
trap one size down.
*Better:* "That's the everybody trap — a position that excludes nobody occupies
nothing. Of your last three customers, which one would have been angriest if
you'd shut down that week, and what did they have that the other two didn't?"

**Write to brief:** Primary segment (header), the hole being taken, the problem
or progress it corresponds to, and the excluded group with a one-line reason.

---

## Step 5 — The move

**Ask:** Given that hole, which of these three moves are you making?

The book gives three, and naming which one is being played prevents the mush
that comes from half-playing all of them:

1. **Be first in a new category.** If you can't be first in the category you're
   in, set up one you can be first in. The category has to be one a customer
   would plausibly go looking for — a category of one that nobody searches for
   is a hole in the wall, not a hole in the mind.
2. **Take the against position.** Avis didn't claim to be better than Hertz; it
   said "We're only No. 2. We try harder," and used the leader's position as
   the thing to hang on. 7-Up became the Uncola by borrowing the cola already
   in the mind. This only works if the leader's position is strong and
   universally known — you're renting their real estate.
3. **Reposition the competition.** To move a new idea in, an old one has to
   move out. Tylenol did it to aspirin by naming who shouldn't take it. This
   is legitimate when the old idea is genuinely wrong or genuinely limited,
   and it's mudslinging when it isn't.

**Then, for whichever move:** what belief has to leave the prospect's head for
yours to fit? Get four or five, and get the uncomfortable ones — too expensive,
too new to trust, we already have something that half-works, switching costs.
These are the objections, and they're the same thing as the old idea in the
mind, seen from the other side.

For each, the honest answer, not the deflection. If the objection is "you're
too new", the answer isn't "we're actually well established", it's what makes
new acceptable here.

**Push back on:** objections they wish people had rather than the ones people
have.

*They say:* "Mostly people think we're too expensive."
*Weak:* "Let's work on how you justify the price." — builds an answer to the
objection people are willing to say out loud rather than the one that decided it.
*Better:* "Price is what people say when they'd rather not explain. What did
the last deal you lost actually say, and what did they use instead?"

**Write to brief:** which move, what has to move out of the mind, and the
objection/response pairs.

---

## Step 6 — The oversimplified message

The book's argument for brevity isn't stylistic. In an overcommunicated
society the mind defends itself by rejecting most of what arrives, and the only
thing that gets through is a message sharpened to a point.

**Ask them to write it, don't write it for them.** Two passes:

**First, one word or a short phrase.** The thing you want to own. *Safety.
Small. The uncola. Overnight.* If they can't get under about five words, the
position from step 4 isn't sharp enough yet — go back rather than padding
forward.

**Then the sentence**, which is what the rest of the workshop consumes:

> For [segment] who [situation], [product] is the [category] that
> [the word from above], unlike [the rung above them].

Let them fill it in first, then tighten together. If you draft it, they'll
accept it and won't be able to defend it an hour later.

Stress-test it three ways:
- Could the leader say this sentence? If yes, it isn't a position.
- Does the segment recognise themselves in it?
- Read it aloud — a person, or a press release?

**Then three supporting points, ranked**, each with evidence attached — a
number, a customer outcome, a demonstration. Ranked, because an unranked list
is what makes homepages read as noise, and because everything below the first
point is what gets cut when space runs out.

This step is worth overrunning; everything downstream depends on it.

**Write to brief:** Value proposition (header), the one word, the rejected
drafts, and the three ranked points with their evidence.

---

## Step 7 — The name

**Ask:** Does your name help this position, fight it, or do nothing?

Ries and Trout treat the name as the hook the brand hangs on in the mind, and
the single most important marketing decision. Three failures to test for:

- **The no-name trap.** Initials only work for companies already famous under
  the full name. A new company using initials is asking the mind to store a
  meaningless string.
- **The line-extension trap.** Putting the established name on a different
  product borrows recognition short-term and blurs the position long-term. The
  book is harder on this than on anything else. If they're planning a second
  product under the same name, this is the moment to say so out loud.
- **The free-ride trap.** A name that leans on someone else's meaning inherits
  their ladder, not their rung.

Renaming is usually off the table and that's fine — the useful output is
knowing whether the name is doing work, is neutral, or is actively costing
them, because the copy flow has to compensate for the last case.

**Write to brief:** the verdict on the name, and anything the copy has to
carry because of it.

---

## Step 8 — Do you match your position?

Chapter 21's last question, and the one that turns a position into something
that can actually be executed. A position stated in a workshop and contradicted
by every page of the site isn't a position.

**Ask:** Name two brands you'd want to sound like, and one you'd hate to sound
like.

Comparisons produce better voice definitions than adjectives. "Professional but
approachable" is meaningless; "like Basecamp, not like Salesforce" is
actionable — and the brands people choose almost always encode the position
they just took in step 6.

Convert to rules that can be followed:
- Three things this voice does (short sentences; concrete nouns; admits
  tradeoffs)
- Three things it never does (exclamation marks; "revolutionary"; hedging)
- Words that are ours, words that are banned
- One before/after: take a sentence from their current site and rewrite it in
  voice, against the position

The before/after is what makes the rest usable. Without it the rules stay
abstract and the copy flow inherits nothing it can act on.

**Write to brief:** voice rules as a list, plus the before/after example.

---

## Closing the flow

Read back the one word and the sentence. Nothing else — the brief holds the
rest.

Then put the book's two remaining questions to them, as questions rather than
steps, because the answers take weeks rather than minutes:

- **Can you afford it?** A position has to be bought as well as chosen. Not
  necessarily in advertising, but in some sustained form of showing up.
- **Can you stick it out?** Positioning is cumulative. The book's recurring
  failure case is the company that abandons a working position out of
  boredom — it has a name, the FWMTS trap: *forgot what made them successful.*
  The position they just wrote is worth more held for two years than
  improved every quarter.

Then the checkpoint: tell them to post their one word and their sentence where
the room can see it. The word is what makes this checkpoint interesting — a
room full of single words shows immediately who found a hole and who is still
in the everybody trap.

Then what's next: the copy flow takes this brief and rewrites their homepage
against it. The first thing it does is check the current page against the
position just built, and the gap is usually large.

## Express pass — ten minutes

Run this when someone arrives at a later flow with no positioning in their
brief. Three questions, no stress-testing. It is deliberately worse than the
full flow; say so, and offer the full version if there's time.

1. **What category does your customer file you under, and who's at the top of
   that ladder?** Take the answer as the category and the main alternative.
   Accept a non-product alternative — a spreadsheet, an intern, nothing — if
   that's what comes back.
2. **What word does that leader own, and what hole is left?** One hole is
   enough. Push once on breadth — the everybody trap at speed is still the
   everybody trap — then take the second answer.
3. **Fill this in:** For [segment] who [situation], [product] is the
   [category] that [the word], unlike [the leader]. Run one test only — could
   the leader say this sentence? — and move on.

**Write to brief:** the header fields it can fill (Product, Category, Primary
segment, Value proposition) and a note that this was the express pass, so the
next person reading it knows it hasn't been stress-tested.

Then go back to the flow they came for.
