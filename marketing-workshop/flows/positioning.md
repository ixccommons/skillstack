# Positioning Flow

Eight steps, roughly 40 minutes. Output is a position the person can defend in a
sentence, plus the voice rules that keep everything downstream consistent.

Run one step per turn. See the pacing section in `SKILL.md` — it matters more
here than anywhere else, because these steps only work if the person is doing the
thinking.

Open by telling them the shape: eight questions, most of the work is in the first
three, and the sentence they're building towards is the one that decides whether
someone chooses them over the alternative.

---

## Step 1 — Product baseline

**Ask:** What does your product do, and who bought it most recently?

The second half is the useful half. "What does it do" produces the rehearsed
pitch; "who bought it last" produces facts. Ask for the last three customers by
name or type if they have them.

**Push back on:** feature lists with no buyer attached. If they describe the
product for two minutes without naming a customer, ask again for the last three.

**Write to brief:** Product (header), plus a short paragraph on current customers.

---

## Step 2 — Segmentation

**Ask:** Of the people who could buy this, which group feels the problem most
sharply — sharply enough to go looking for a solution?

A segment is not a demographic. "Small businesses" and "marketing teams" are
categories, not segments. A segment is a group that shares a situation and a
pain: *agency owners billing hourly who just lost a retainer client*, *solo
ecommerce operators doing their own fulfilment past 50 orders a day*.

**Push back on:** breadth. Breadth feels safe and is the most common failure at
this step. If they name three segments, ask which one they'd keep if they could
only serve one for a year.

**Then ask the cut:** who are you explicitly *not* for? A position that excludes
nobody isn't a position. Get at least one named group they'll turn away.

**Write to brief:** Primary segment (header), secondary segments, and the
excluded group with a one-line reason.

---

## Step 3 — Competitors and category

**Ask:** When someone decides not to buy from you, what do they do instead?

This is a better question than "who are your competitors" because it surfaces the
real alternatives — a spreadsheet, an intern, doing nothing — which are usually
what's actually winning. Get four or five, and include at least one non-product
alternative.

Then, for each: what does that alternative do well? Not "why they're worse" —
what a customer genuinely gets from choosing it. People are bad at this and it's
where the honest analysis lives.

If the person doesn't know the landscape, search for it rather than working from
memory — competitor sets change fast, and naming a company that pivoted or shut
down in front of a room costs you credibility.

**Write to brief:** Category (header), plus the alternatives table with what each
does well.

---

## Step 4 — The gap

**Ask:** Looking at that list, what does everyone claim, and what does nobody
clearly own?

Read the alternatives back and find the shared vocabulary. If four competitors
all say "powerful yet simple", that ground is taken and worthless. The gap is
something true about them that no competitor is saying loudly.

Two tests before accepting a gap:
- Would a competitor argue with it? If nobody would dispute it, it isn't a
  position, it's a platitude.
- Is it true today, or aspirational? Aspirational gaps produce copy that
  overpromises and support tickets that follow.

**Push back on:** gaps that are really feature differences. "We have a better
API" is a feature; "the only one an ops team can run without engineering" is a
position.

**Write to brief:** The gap, plus the crowded ground being avoided.

---

## Step 5 — Value proposition

**Ask them to write it, don't write it for them.** One sentence:

> For [segment] who [situation/pain], [product] is the [category] that
> [differentiator], unlike [main alternative].

Let them fill it in first, then tighten together. If you draft it, they'll accept
it and won't be able to defend it an hour later.

Then stress-test:
- Could a competitor say the same sentence? If yes, it's not differentiated.
- Does the segment recognise themselves in it?
- Read it aloud — does it sound like a person or like a press release?

Iterate until it survives all three. This step is worth overrunning; it's the one
the rest of the day depends on.

**Write to brief:** Value proposition (header), plus the two or three rejected
drafts — those are useful later when someone wants to know what was considered.

---

## Step 6 — Messaging hierarchy

**Ask:** If you get three supporting points under that sentence, what are they?

Rank them. Most people have five or six things they want to say and no order,
which is why their homepage reads as a list. Force the ranking: first, second,
third, and everything else goes below the fold or in a different asset entirely.

Each point needs evidence attached — a number, a customer outcome, a
demonstration. A claim without evidence is a claim the reader discounts.

**Write to brief:** Three ranked points, each with its evidence.

---

## Step 7 — Objections

**Ask:** What's the last thing someone thinks before deciding not to buy?

Get four or five. The useful ones are uncomfortable: too expensive, too small a
company to trust, we already have something that half-works, switching costs.

For each, write the honest answer — not the deflection. If the objection is
"you're too new", the answer isn't "actually we're very established", it's what
makes new acceptable here. Deflections read as evasive and get quoted back at
you.

**Push back on:** objections they wish people had instead of the ones people
actually have. Ask what the last lost deal said.

**Write to brief:** Objection and response, paired.

---

## Step 8 — Brand voice

**Ask:** Name two brands you'd want to sound like, and one you'd hate to sound
like.

Comparisons produce better voice definitions than adjectives do. "Professional
but approachable" is meaningless; "like Basecamp, not like Salesforce" is
actionable.

Then convert to rules that can actually be followed:
- Three things this voice does (short sentences; concrete nouns; admits
  tradeoffs)
- Three things it never does (exclamation marks; "revolutionary"; hedging)
- Words that are ours, words that are banned
- One before/after: take a sentence from their current site and rewrite it in
  voice

The before/after is the part that makes the rest usable. Without it the rules
stay abstract.

**Write to brief:** Voice rules as a list, plus the before/after example.

---

## Closing the flow

Read back the value proposition and the three supporting points — nothing else.
The brief holds the rest.

Then the checkpoint: tell them to post their value proposition where the room can
see it.

Then tell them what's next: the copy flow takes this brief and rewrites their
homepage against it. If they're continuing straight on, note that the first thing
copy will do is check their current homepage against the position they just
built, and the gap is usually large.

---

## Express pass — ten minutes

Run this when someone arrives at the copy or distribution flow with no
positioning section in their brief. Three questions, three answers, no
stress-testing. It is deliberately worse than the full flow; say so, and offer the
full version if there's time.

1. **Who bought most recently, and what did they have in common?** Take the
   answer as the segment. Push once on breadth — "small businesses" still isn't a
   segment at speed — then accept whatever comes back second.
2. **What do people do instead of buying from you?** One answer is enough, and
   the non-product alternative is the more useful one if they offer it.
3. **Fill this in:** For [segment] who [pain], [product] is the [category] that
   [differentiator], unlike [alternative]. Run one test only — could a competitor
   say this sentence? — and move on.

**Write to brief:** the header fields (Product, Category, Primary segment, Value
proposition) and a note that this was the express pass, so the next person
reading it knows it hasn't been stress-tested.

Then go back to the flow they came for.
